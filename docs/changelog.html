Changelog

Every version, in a line or two.

Unreleased in development

The plugin pivot

Freally MIDI Master becomes a CLAP plugin, with VST3 and AU projected from it. The desktop app is retired.

  • The interface can fall over without taking your session with it — if something in the window breaks, you now get a panel with a way back into your work instead of a dead rectangle inside your project that only removing and re-inserting the plugin could clear. Your arrangement lives outside the part that broke, so pressing the button redraws what you had. What happened is written to a crashes folder on your own disk — the audio engine, a hardware fault and the interface each write their own file, and the newest twenty are kept. Nothing is sent anywhere; it is there so you have something to attach to a bug report if you want to.
  • Drag the whole arrangement and the cursor shows the arrangement — the picture riding on the pointer was a graph, which told you nothing about the record you were holding. It is a miniature of it now: one row per part, each section’s clip at the bar it is going to land on, so you recognise your own song before you let go. Hold Ctrl (Command on macOS) and it shows them stacked instead, which is where they will actually go.
  • The stems panel shows up when you generate a song — it appeared only if you happened to have generated a single part earlier in the same session, which is why it seemed to come and go for no reason. Generating an arrangement asks for it directly now.
  • A pedal hat and a ride bell are audible — four artists asked for them in their research and were being silently refused, so the layer was written and never heard: John Mayer and Maroon 5 wanted a pedal hat, Chris Dave and Robert Glasper a ride bell. All four play now. A style that names a percussion voice the engine cannot write is refused when the dataset is built, rather than going quiet on the grid.
  • Any drum row opens into a pitch lane — trap lives on 808s that move, and a grid that draws them as on/off cannot say what they are playing. Open a row and it becomes seven — the lane’s root, three semitones either side — and a hit is something you drag to a pitch rather than a value the grid threw away. Drag past the edge and the window follows, so the reach is the whole of MIDI while the row stays seven tall. Every lane, not just the 808: on the 808 the rows are notes, everywhere else they are the sample transposed, so a pitched hat roll or a tuned tom fill is the same gesture. Hits outside the window are marked rather than hidden, and the whole drag is one Ctrl+Z.
  • The sample browser holds a real library — a folder with two thousand files in it opens as fast as one with four, because only the rows you can see are drawn. Type to filter and the tree narrows to what matches, keeping the folders that lead to it — and it says which folders it searched, because it can only look inside the ones you have opened. Unplug the drive a library folder lives on and its tab says so, with a Check again beside it, instead of a folder that silently refuses to open.
  • Hear a MIDI file before you decide what to do with it — press Play on a .mid and it sounds, through a plain built-in instrument rather than whichever artist you have selected, so the same file sounds the same tomorrow. Drum lanes come through as hits rather than pitches. It never touches your project’s transport.
  • Every take you have ever made, browsable — the take counter opens an index of your generations grouped by artist, each one saying how long it is, how fast, and when you made it. Pick one and it comes back. The arrows answer “back one”; this is for the beat from Tuesday night whose seed you never wrote down. It is kept per user, survives an update, and each artist keeps its own run so an evening on one cannot push out another’s.
  • The artist pane says what a style does not write — along with its tempo range and its moods by name. An artist who does not write countermelodies answers that tab with silence, correctly — and now says so, instead of leaving you to press Generate and stare at an empty roll.
  • Drag the preview waveform to scrub — the browser’s transport already seeked on a click; now the audio follows your finger, which is how you find the start of a transient without clicking twenty times.
  • 52 genres — up from twenty. Dark plugg, sexy drill, rage, jersey club, amapiano, afroswing, baile funk, jungle, neurofunk, UK garage, hyphy and detroit bounce among them, each one written from research rather than tagged onto a preset.
  • Drag a box across the drum grid — hold Shift and drag, and every cell the box touches is selected, not just the ones the pointer crossed. Then Ctrl+C, Ctrl+V, Ctrl+D and Delete work on the lot. A copied triplet comes back as a triplet rather than being flattened onto the grid, and right-click and drag wipes every cell it crosses in one gesture that one Ctrl+Z puts back.
  • The drum lane names are readable — the lock and the fill button now appear when you reach for a row, and give the name back the room it needs. It had been rendering as a single letter. A locked padlock stays visible whatever the pointer is doing, because a lock you cannot see is a lock you forget you set.
  • The keyboard shortcuts panel has a button, next to Settings and About — a panel whose whole job is telling you what the keys do was reachable only by pressing a key. It fits on one screen now, four columns wide instead of one long scroll, and the drum grid’s own gestures are in it.
  • Every generated note is visible, up and down as well as across — a melody spanning two octaves had notes above and below the rows on screen. The roll sizes its rows to what was written and centres it.
  • The loop brace resizes by its edges — the grip took the first handle in range rather than the nearest, so a short loop’s right edge could not be grabbed at all, and missing it drew a new loop over the one you were aiming at.
  • Eight drum pads across the top of the stage — each one a lane, with its name, what is on it, and a green or red dot saying whether you can hear it. Press the pad to mute it, press Play in its middle to hear that sound alone, drag a sample onto it from the browser, shuffle it for a new one from the folder you are in, or clear it back to the built-in. Every pad’s name is a picker over all thirty-seven lanes, two pads may share one so you can layer a snare, and the layout is remembered per artist.
  • One box finds anybody — the search field, the genre chips and the five-hundred-row roster are replaced by a single type-to-autocomplete box that knows artists, genres, aliases and typos. Stop typing halfway and click away and it takes the best match, so you can never be left with nothing chosen. The artist’s details now sit directly underneath it rather than below a list you had to scroll past to read them, and Original Workflow is pinned to the top whatever is selected.
  • The whole pattern is on screen — the piano roll used to open at a fixed zoom, so anything past four bars ran off the right-hand edge and had to be zoomed out by hand after every generation. It now fits whatever was generated, four bars or eight.
  • Mute a generator from its own tab — the switches were a second row carrying the same six words as the tabs, so working out which one silenced Drums meant reading “Drums” twice. Each tab now has its own dot.
  • The window behaves — it can be dragged, resized and maximised, and the interface scales with it instead of leaving a dead margin or being cropped. On a scaled display the plugin and its interface finally agree on how big a pixel is, which is what caused both.
  • A style you built comes back with its sounds — the samples a style copied were being kept and never loaded, so the promise that they survive you moving the originals was not true. It is now.
  • “Single hit” keeps the note — the one menu item whose name promises a note was deleting it. It collapses a roll back to a single hit, which is what it always said it did.
  • Every artist and genre has moods now — twelve genres had none, and each gained three: boom bap’s dusty / jazzy / hard, phonk’s cowbell / memphis / brazilian, country’s shuffle / ballad / barnburner. Pressing Generate walks an artist’s range instead of returning one sound with a different seed on it — 96 artist-and-mood pairs across the whole roster, up from 33.
  • Build a style of your own — start from any artist or genre, set the tempo range, swing, hat density, melody density and scales, and save it. It appears in the roster marked Yours and generates, locks, re-rolls and exports exactly like a shipped one, because it is one. It inherits everything you did not change, including improvements the original gets later.
  • Train it on the takes you keep — star a generation, and at thirty the app fits a style to what you kept. There is no machine learning in it: it measures your takes and writes the numbers back, offline, and a style that would only repeat itself is refused rather than saved. Your own MIDI files can train it too.
  • Nothing copies your samples without asking — a style can keep the one-shots you assigned so they survive you moving the originals, and because that means a second copy on your drive it tells you how many files and how many megabytes first. The box is unticked, and saving a style copies nothing on its own.
  • Re-roll a pad from the folder you are browsing — a dice on any lane pulls a new sample matched to what that lane is, so a snare gets snares, and one dice re-rolls the whole kit. Locked pads are left alone. Kits can be named and saved too, and come back in any song.
  • Your samples sound better — a sample recorded at a different rate from the kit around it used to be stretched to fit on every single note. It is converted once now, properly filtered, when you load it.
  • The genre chips said they could not be clicked — the Genres row showed a “no entry” cursor over controls that worked perfectly, and could not show which genre was selected. Both fixed.
  • Your pattern library cannot lose a take, and cannot take the DAW with it — two patterns whose names differ only in punctuation used to become one file, so saving the second silently deleted the first; and one unreadable file could crash the host instead of simply being skipped. Saving over your own name still replaces it, which is the point.
  • A locked lane stays locked — in the project, in the export, and in your takes — it now survives saving and reopening, it no longer carries notes past the end of a clip you shortened, it holds through Generate All, and stepping back through your takes gives you that take rather than a blend of it and whatever is locked now, at the tempo and key it was actually written at.
  • Soloing a drum row solos a drum row — it used to silence the melody, countermelody, bass and chords too, with no control in any of those editors to show why or undo it.
  • Song Mode screens its melodies — the novelty guard ran on the Melody tab and not on arrangements, so a whole song was written without it.
  • Hi-hat fills land where they should — a hand-added fill sat 40 ticks ahead of the beat, and in 6/8 it cleared twice as much of the bar as it should have. A fill can no longer put a closed hat on top of a sounding open one either.
  • A novelty guard, so a melody is not a hook somebody already owns — every melody and countermelody is screened before you hear it, and a take that matches a known contour is thrown away and drawn again. Nothing in the table is a melody: it holds one-way fingerprints of contours, and there is no way back from one to a note. Transposing a hook does not hide it, and playing it staccato does not either.
  • Every genre has its own voice — NY drill’s bells, phonk’s cowbell lead, pluggnb’s chord-tone arps and west coast’s four-note riff are each written from the research instead of falling back on one shared default. Six genres with no 808 got a real bassline to go with it.
  • Hi-hat fills — the hat is where trap, drill and plugg do their talking, and now it does: a roll, a stutter, a triplet burst, a rising ramp, or the hat stopping dead, landing where the genre says. One press adds one at the phrase end.
  • Lock a drum so a re-roll cannot touch it — click the padlock on any lane, or press L with the row focused, and Generate rebuilds everything else around it note for note.
  • A pattern library that outlives the project — name something you made and it is there next time, in any song and any DAW. Saved as notes, with no kit, so you can load it and put whatever sounds you like underneath.
  • Every take you have made this session — ◀ and ▶ walk your generations, counted per generator from the first, and stepping back brings the whole setup: the artist, the mood, the seed, the bars and the pins. It shows the tempo and key that were actually used, which is not always what you pinned.
  • Sixteen more drums — sub kick, ghost snare, pedal hat, ride bell, high and low toms, clave, conga, bongo, timbale, triangle, a second perc, an 808 sub layer, and riser / impact / reverse. Every row names its instrument, and any slot can be switched to a drum the kit is not already using.
  • Solo and audition, on every drum lane — solo one and the rest go quiet while your notes still reach your DAW untouched, and clicking a lane’s name plays that drum on its own so you can hear which pad you are about to edit.
  • A roll palette on every step — right-click any cell and pick 2, 3, 4, 6 or 8 hits inside it.
  • Click the piano roll’s ruler to move the playhead — the drum grid and the song timeline already did; the four melodic generators had no way to move the transport at all.
  • More keys, and the controls say what they are — G generates, Shift+G generates every part, R re-rolls, and 1–6 pick a generator.
  • The roster says what something is before you generate — a badge for artist or genre, and a pane giving the era, the genres and what that artist tends to do: tempo, key, half-time, how many moods.
  • A real drum kit — off-snare, ride, crash, tom, shaker, tambourine, cowbell and woodblock join the kick, snare, clap, hats, rim, snap, perc and sub. Which of them an artist uses is part of that artist, and fifteen of the shipped styles had already been written with a percussion block that nothing had ever read.
  • A kit per genre — eleven families, each with its own tuning, top end and drive. Boom-bap stops at 8 kHz because that is what an SP-1200 could sample; drill’s sub slides further; country is open and undriven. Styles had been naming the kit they wanted for months and all of them played trap samples.
  • Generate varies the take without changing the record — press it on the drums, switch to the melody, press it again, and the new melody is written against the same key and chords as the drums. Before this the second press quietly drew an unrelated harmony.
  • A sample browser — add your sample folders once and they come back with the project. Browse them and drop a sample straight onto a drum lane.
  • A keyboard-shortcuts panel on ? or F1, grouped by what they do — and Space plays and pauses, which it did not before.
  • Play the generators together, or any one alone — each generated part gets a switch beside the tabs, and the transport moved to the top beside them.
  • The plugin plays without the DAW rolling — auditioning a beat no longer means arming a track and starting the whole project. Starting the DAW’s transport takes it straight back.
  • 808 slides are audible — the generators have written them for a long time and every rendered WAV played them flat.
  • Drag a part onto a DAW track — click a part's MIDI or Audio chip and you get a menu of every instrument playing in it, so you can drag just the hats, or All Tracks to take every lane out at once as separate files. Hold Ctrl as you drop to stack them instead of laying them end to end. The whole arrangement drags too, now as audio as well as MIDI. Files arrive named trap - Snare - 140 BPM - C# Minor, and the clip's own notes ride on the cursor so you can see what you picked up. Windows is the one that has actually been dropped into Ableton; macOS and Linux have their own native drag sources now, switched on but not yet proven in a DAW.
  • Generate really does generate — pressing it repeatedly used to return the same beat, because the seed the engine picked was echoed into the box and then sent back. The box now tells apart a seed you chose from one it is merely showing you, and the padlock says which.
  • Dropped audio arrives at the right tempo — a dragged loop carried no tempo at all, so Ableton warped it by guess. It now carries the tempo, beat count and meter the way loop libraries do.
  • Move clips around the arrangement — every other DAW gesture was already there, so rearranging meant copy, paste, then delete the original.
  • Transpose from the keyboard — Ctrl+↑/↓ for a semitone, Shift+↑/↓ for an octave, on one note or a whole selection.
  • Stems, per part or per lane, as MIDI or as audio — the drums on their own, or one file per drum lane, rendered through whatever kit is actually playing.
  • Your own one-shot on any part — drums lane by lane, plus melody, countermelody, bassline and chords, from WAV, AIFF, FLAC, MP3, M4A or OGG.
  • The drum grid is an editor — click to place or clear a hit, Alt+click to clone the previous bar, Ctrl+2…9 for a tuplet, Delete to clear.
  • Every artist writes their own snare roll and their own harmony — the roll used to be one hardcoded shape shared across the roster, and twelve genres shared one set of chords.
  • Song Mode — pick an artist, press Generate, and get a whole arrangement: one of their own song forms, a clip per part per section, and the transitions that make it read as a record rather than a loop repeated.
  • An arrangement you edit before it leaves — a ruler with bar numbers and timestamps, clips you can select, resize, clone, delete and copy/paste, a note-density sketch inside every one, and locks on any cell, row or section.
  • Songs play — the marker runs across the whole record, click anywhere to seek, loop any section, and mute or solo a part while you work.
  • Re-roll one section — without touching the rest, keeping whatever you locked. A locked chord part is what the new melody gets written against.
  • Open any clip in its editor — double-click a cell to drill into the piano roll or the pad grid, and your edits write back into the arrangement.
  • Export the song — one multi-track MIDI file, or one file per part into a folder, through your platform's own Save As.
  • Undo covers the arrangement — one Ctrl+Z, one stack, whichever tab you are on, and it reaches the saved project.
  • The transport plays what you are looking at — leaving Song Mode hands it back the clip on screen, and muting or soloing a part does not restart the record.
  • The plugin makes a sound — the preview kit is compiled in and rendered into the host's output, so a generated beat is audible on insert.
  • MIDI-only and per-lane mute — silence the plugin's own kit, or one lane of it, while the notes keep reaching your DAW.
  • A running playhead — moves with the tempo, click the grid to seek, Pause holds and Stop returns to the start.
  • All five generators — drums, melody, countermelody, bass and chords, generated in dependency order.
  • Genre ↔ artist cross-filtering — pick a genre to narrow the roster, or an artist to see the genres it works in.
  • Melody & countermelody generators — phrase structures, chord-tone bias, per-genre devices.
  • Moods — named overrides per artist, merged before generation.
  • Presets — six factory presets compiled in, plus your own.
  • Unlimited undo/redo — every session change, one keystroke back.
  • Linux editor — the plugin window now opens over X11 and WebKitGTK.
  • Windows standalone renders — a message-filter bug fixed in the editor loop.
  • The desktop shell's code is removed — with it go the updater and the crash reporter, so the plugin now makes no outbound connections at all.
  • Loads and generates in Ableton Live 12 (VST3) and FL Studio (CLAP).
0.2.0 25 July 2026

The last desktop build

Search an artist, generate drums, hear it, drag it out. Retired on 29 July 2026 — it stays up as the last of the desktop app.

  • Drum generation across the full roster, with humanize and swing.
  • Fuzzy artist search, genre chips, and the session chips.
  • Audio preview and MIDI drag-out.
0.1.0 22 July 2026

First cut

The dataset, the engine and the studio shell — the skeleton everything since has hung on.

  • Style dataset with inheritance, and the deterministic drum engine.
  • The three-pane studio layout, dark and light themes, 18 locales.
